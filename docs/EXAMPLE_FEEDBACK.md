# Example essay feedback

An illustrative student, situation and draft, created to demonstrate useful feedback on an everyday experience. No real student’s work or testimonial is represented. Feedback is prewritten for demonstration, not a live AI response. Suggestions are questions and revision options; they do not supply new experiences or a replacement essay.

## Prompt

Describe a topic, idea, or concept you find so engaging that it makes you lose all track of time. Why does it captivate you? What or who do you turn to when you want to learn more?

## Draft

A classmate once asked me what 随便 meant. I said “whatever.” A few days later, she showed me a message she was about to send. Someone had asked where she wanted to eat, and she wanted to say she was happy with anything. She asked whether “whatever” sounded annoyed. I wasn't sure how to explain why it might.

I had answered her first question quickly because it seemed easy. Then I tried the reply “whatever” in my head a few different ways. Some sounded relaxed. Others sounded as if I wanted the conversation to end. I knew the difference when I heard it, but I couldn't give her a rule that covered all of them.

Since then, I have started saving small expressions in my phone: “that's fine,” “if you want,” “never mind.” Sometimes I put two versions of a message next to each other and ask a friend which one sounds more natural. We don't always agree. Once we spent part of lunch discussing whether a full stop made a short reply seem unfriendly. By the time we stopped, I had barely touched the homework I had brought downstairs.

I usually check a learner's dictionary first, then ask classmates who use the expression. I also pause videos to compare a line with its subtitles. A dictionary helps with the meaning, but I still have to work out what the person is doing with the words. I sometimes get distracted collecting examples instead of figuring out what I think about them.

What keeps me interested is that the same few words can do different things between different people. I like trying to work out which detail changes the meaning. It can be frustrating when I think I have understood something and the next example doesn't fit.

Now, when someone asks me for a translation, I usually ask what they want to say first. That takes longer than supplying one English word. I still catch myself giving the quick answer sometimes.

## Overall response

This draft makes a small misunderstanding interesting by showing how it stayed with you. The lunch discussion and unfinished homework give the interest a believable place in your day. The strongest idea is the gap between knowing a translation and understanding how it will sound to another person. The main gap is what you actually learned from examining an example: paragraphs three and four describe collecting opinions, but do not yet let us follow one comparison. Develop that exchange before adding another anecdote or polishing the ending.

### What to preserve

> I wasn't sure how to explain why it might.

The opening gives you a small, credible reason to become curious. You admit that your first answer was incomplete without making the mistake into a dramatic turning point. Keep that proportion.

> By the time we stopped, I had barely touched the homework I had brought downstairs.

Lunch and the neglected homework show the interest taking up time naturally. Keep this detail: it shows the interest competing with something else you meant to do.

> I sometimes get distracted collecting examples instead of figuring out what I think about them.

This admission gives the learning process a useful complication: collecting examples is not the same as understanding them. Keep it if it accurately describes the habit; you can explore it without turning it into a resolved weakness.

### First priority: Let us follow one comparison, not just the habit

Use the lunch conversation or the opening translation question to show your thinking at work. What did you initially think a reply meant? What detail made you or your friend read it differently? What did you conclude, or leave unresolved? A short exchange can connect the scene, your learning process and the reason you keep returning to the topic. Let that exchange carry the reflection. If you cannot remember the exact words, summarize the discussion accurately rather than creating a quotation.

### Response to the prompt

The topic is clear: how short expressions carry different meanings in different situations. The lunch detail shows you losing track of another task, and the final paragraph suggests a change in how you help someone. The learning sources are named as broad types, but the draft needs one example of what a dictionary entry, classmate or subtitle helped you reconsider. Strengthening that connection will also make the explanation of why the topic captivates you less general.

## Diagnostic areas

### Response to the prompt

**developing** — You show the interest, some lost time and ways of learning. The remaining gap is a concrete connection between a source or conversation and a change in your understanding.

> I usually check a learner's dictionary first, then ask classmates who use the expression.

**Next step:** After one learning example, explain what you thought before it and what you thought afterward, including any uncertainty. Check that this also clarifies why you wanted to continue.

### Specificity

**strong foundation** — The message about lunch, phone notes and unfinished homework give the essay a recognizable setting. A useful new detail would clarify the comparison, rather than decorate the scene.

> Once we spent part of lunch discussing whether a full stop made a short reply seem unfriendly.

**Next step:** Add the relevant context around one expression: who was speaking, what they were trying to say, and which wording prompted different readings. Keep identities and private message details out if they are unnecessary.

### Reflection

**developing** — The draft recognizes a difference between translating words and helping someone communicate. It also admits that your examples do not always fit. The next step is to show what this tension leads you to think or ask.

> It can be frustrating when I think I have understood something and the next example doesn't fit.

**Next step:** Develop one failed explanation or disagreement. Say which part of your original view you kept, changed or could not resolve; you do not need to present a universal rule.

### Structure and pacing

**developing** — The opening sets up one translation problem, then the middle moves through phone notes, punctuation, dictionaries and subtitles. Each is relevant, but together they spread attention across several examples before one is developed.

> I also pause videos to compare a line with its subtitles.

**Next step:** Choose the opening exchange or lunch discussion as the main thread. Keep the other learning methods brief, and develop the one that genuinely helped with that thread.

### Clarity

**developing** — The opening shifts from the Chinese expression to the English word “whatever.” The classmate wants an English reply, but readers may initially wonder which language the message uses. Later, “different things” leaves the central idea broad.

> A few days later, she showed me a message she was about to send.

**Next step:** Clarify that the classmate is choosing an English reply. Then use the chosen comparison to explain the relevant distinction, without treating either language as having one fixed tone.

### Voice

**strong foundation** — The uncertainty, unfinished homework and occasional quick answer make the narrator approachable. You do not need to replace this voice with technical terms to justify the interest.

> I still catch myself giving the quick answer sometimes.

**Next step:** Read the new explanation aloud beside the opening. Keep phrasing you would naturally use, including uncertainty when it is accurate. Explain any specialist term you genuinely need.

### Opening and ending

**developing** — The ending returns to helping someone translate and keeps the change modest. It will feel more earned if the middle shows how you came to ask about intention rather than supply a single word.

> Now, when someone asks me for a translation, I usually ask what they want to say first.

**Next step:** Develop the middle first. Then check whether the last paragraph reflects what you actually learned. Keep the unfinished habit if it is true; a polished moral or perfect transformation is unnecessary.

### Concision

**developing** — The list of saved expressions establishes a habit, but the draft also lists several learning methods. These compete with space that could show one actual comparison.

> Since then, I have started saving small expressions in my phone: “that's fine,” “if you want,” “never mind.”

**Next step:** Keep one or two expressions and the methods that help explain your main example. Use the space for reasoning, not more accomplishments. The 650-word limit is a ceiling, not a target to fill.

## Passage-by-passage feedback

### 1. High priority

> Once we spent part of lunch discussing whether a full stop made a short reply seem unfriendly.

**Observation:** This is a useful scene, but we do not yet know what the two of you compared or why you read the reply differently. The next sentence shows lost time more clearly than this one shows the thinking.

**Why it matters:** The scene can answer both why the topic holds your attention and how another person helps you learn. One developed exchange would do more work than several additional examples.

**Revision question:** What was the short reply, if you remember it? What did each of you think the full stop changed? Which part of the disagreement made you reconsider something, or remained unresolved? Summarize if you cannot recall the exact words.

### 2. High priority

> I usually check a learner's dictionary first, then ask classmates who use the expression.

**Observation:** You tell us the types of sources you consult, but not what one source added. A dictionary and a classmate may help with different parts of the same question.

**Why it matters:** The prompt asks what or whom you turn to when you want to learn. Explaining one useful idea and your response would show how you learn.

**Revision question:** Choose a source or conversation you actually remember. What question did you bring to it, what did it help explain, and what was still unclear? Name or describe the source accurately so a reader can follow the learning.

### 3. Medium priority

> What keeps me interested is that the same few words can do different things between different people.

**Observation:** This states the central idea, but “different things” is broader than the specific situations in the opening and lunch scene.

**Why it matters:** A reader needs to understand what interests you about the difference. You can explain that through the example you choose, without turning an observation into a rule for every speaker.

**Revision question:** In your main example, what were the two possible readings? What made it interesting that both seemed plausible to somebody? Which detail did you want to understand better? Keep the explanation tied to that situation.

### 4. Medium priority

> A few days later, she showed me a message she was about to send.

**Observation:** The scene moves from explaining a Chinese expression to choosing an English reply. That transition is understandable in context, but it asks the reader to infer the language of the message.

**Why it matters:** A small clarification would let readers follow the question immediately and spend their attention on your thinking. It would also avoid suggesting that the original expression and “whatever” always have matching uses.

**Revision question:** Can you briefly clarify that she was drafting an English reply and what she wanted it to communicate? Keep only the context needed to understand the exchange; the real recipient or private message does not need to appear.

### 5. Medium priority

> I sometimes get distracted collecting examples instead of figuring out what I think about them.

**Observation:** This complication makes the learning feel less tidy. It also opens a useful question about the difference between noticing examples and making sense of one.

**Why it matters:** Keeping a limitation can make the reflection more precise. You do not have to claim you fixed the habit, but the reader would benefit from knowing how you think about it now.

**Revision question:** Can you recall a time when another example complicated your explanation? What made you stop, compare or ask a better question, if anything? If the habit is still unresolved, say so without adding a neat solution.

### 6. Low priority

> I still catch myself giving the quick answer sometimes.

**Observation:** The final sentence resists a perfect before-and-after story. It can work because it returns to the quick answer in the opening.

**Why it matters:** An ending does not need to announce a career goal or erase uncertainty. Its job here is to leave us with an accurate sense of how you now approach a familiar situation.

**Revision question:** After expanding the middle, does this still feel like the most honest place to stop? Keep it if so. If another ending fits better, use a real question or observation rather than adding a new achievement.

## Revision plan

1. **Choose the opening exchange or lunch discussion as the main example. Note what each person meant, the wording discussed and what you remember accurately. Done when you can separate remembered detail from a guess.**
   A reliable example gives you material for the revision. You do not need to reconstruct private messages or make up dialogue.

2. **Expand that example with your initial reading, the other reading and what you reconsidered. Done when a reader can follow the comparison and understand why you wanted to keep thinking about it.**
   This connects the scene to the intellectual curiosity the prompt asks about. The outcome may remain uncertain.

3. **Connect one actual learning source or conversation to that example. Done when the reader can identify the question you asked, what you learned and what remained unclear. Keep other source types brief.**
   This answers how you learn, rather than simply listing where you look. A classmate can be a useful source when you explain what their perspective added.

4. **Clarify the English-message context, then trim examples that repeat the same point. Done when each remaining detail either helps us follow the exchange or adds something to your thinking.**
   The clarification removes an avoidable ambiguity; the cuts make room for the comparison without lengthening every paragraph.

5. **Read the revised middle beside the opening and ending. Done when they describe a believable, still-developing habit, every prompt part is addressed, and all details are supportable. Check the 650-word ceiling last.**
   This protects the natural voice and modest scale of the experience. No dramatic transformation or major declaration is necessary.

## Voice and evidence checks

- **Would you explain the lunch conversation this way to a friend?** Keep your ordinary phrasing when expanding it. Use the added detail to help us follow who understood what, and why.
- **Are the message, source and remembered words accurate enough to use?** Paraphrase when exact wording is uncertain. Keep unnecessary private details out, and do not invent a dictionary entry, video title or conversation.
- **Does the revision still allow you to be unsure?** The disagreement and occasional quick answer are part of this draft’s voice. Check that your explanation leaves room for both.

## Questions for the writer

- Which of the two conversations do you remember most clearly?
- What did the other person notice that you had missed?
- Which actual source or conversation helped you think through that difference?
- What do you still find difficult about helping someone choose words?

## Limits

This is an illustrative, prewritten sample, not a real student’s submission, testimonial or live AI response. Feedback cannot verify memories or sources. Questions suggest directions to explore; they are not facts to add. No view is offered on admission chances or how this essay fits a complete application. The writer should keep control of the experiences, interpretation and wording.
